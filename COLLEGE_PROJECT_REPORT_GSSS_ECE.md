# ABSTRACT

*(Formulated strictly in three paragraphs of 5–6 lines each as prescribed by the GSSS ECE circular)*

Modern multicore Systems-on-Chip (SoCs) and tile-based Manycore Processor architectures rely on Networks-on-Chip (NoCs) as the primary communications backbone, making the on-chip interconnection network a high-value vulnerability target for malicious microarchitectural hardware trojans. Hardware trojans operating within router control logic—such as Blackhole, Selective Forwarding, Grayhole, Routing Manipulation, and Destination Tampering—can systematically disrupt packet flow, silently corrupt flit headers, or induce catastrophic denial-of-service across processing elements. Furthermore, comparative cybersecurity evaluations in standard discrete-event simulators are frequently corrupted by pseudo-random number generator (RNG) coupling between router arbitration and packet generation, which shifts global traffic phases and produces deceptive evaluation baselines.

To overcome these architectural vulnerabilities, this project presents **NoC Sentinel**, an open-source, evidence-grounded cybersecurity and fault-tolerant mitigation platform developed within the cycle-accurate SystemC/Noxim simulation environment. Sentinel introduces a mathematically provable Breadth-First Search (BFS) avoidance routing protocol on the punctured mesh graph $G \setminus \{N\}$ that guarantees deadlock- and loop-freedom by enforcing strictly decreasing topological distance to destinations without illegal diagonal hops. In addition, Sentinel integrates a decoupled $xorshift32$ pseudo-random generator that segregates router arbitration from packet generation, achieving 100% pairwise traffic injection invariance across clean and attacked simulation runs, alongside a configurable ingress/egress policy engine supporting source quarantine and authenticated backup destination remapping.

The platform was comprehensively evaluated across an exhaustive 63-run test matrix on a $4 \times 4$ 2D mesh utilizing the verified Accellera SystemC 2.3.1 static library, demonstrating 100.0% settled delivery recovery across all five trojan prototypes with zero routing loops and zero illegal visits to compromised routers. The study transparently discloses a critical scale-dependent domain shift in a pre-trained 15-feature Random Forest detector (100% false positive rate on $4 \times 4$ runs caused by tree splits on absolute packet volume features), provides an exact flit-level accounting reconciliation of warm-up and drain intervals, and deploys an interactive Streamlit intelligence dashboard integrated with Google Gemini for grounded AI threat briefings, delivering an auditable, publication-grade cybersecurity platform for safety-critical multicore SoCs.

# CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background and Overview of Networks-on-Chip

The rapid advancement of semiconductor manufacturing technologies has enabled the integration of dozens to hundreds of processing cores, specialized accelerators, and memory controllers onto a single silicon die, giving rise to modern Multiprocessor Systems-on-Chip (MPSoCs) [1]. In early generation MPSoCs, communication between processing elements (PEs) was orchestrated via shared multidrop buses or crossbar switches. However, as core counts scaled beyond 16 cores, shared bus architectures encountered severe physical limitations, including capacitive wire loading, exponential arbitration latency, severe bandwidth bottlenecks, and excessive power dissipation [2].

To overcome the scalability limits of shared buses, the **Network-on-Chip (NoC)** paradigm emerged as the universally adopted communication backbone for high-performance multicore processors, graphic processing units (GPUs), and artificial intelligence (AI) accelerators [1, 2]. As depicted in Figure 1.1, an NoC replaces ad-hoc wiring with a structured, packet-switched micro-network composed of routers, bidirectional physical links, and Network Interfaces (NIs) arranged in regular geometric topologies, most predominantly the two-dimensional (2D) Mesh [1]. Data packets generated by cores are segmented into fixed-size flow control units (**flits**)—comprising head, body, and tail flits—and routed across intermediate routers using dimension-order protocols such as XY routing [2].

```

|                  GLOBAL SEMICONDUCTOR SUPPLY CHAIN                      |
|                                                                         |
|  [3rd-Party IP Vendor] ---> [SoC Design House] ---> [Outsourced Foundry]|

|   (Blackhole/Grayhole)        (Misdirection/Loops)         (Tampering)  |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+
```
*Figure 1.1: Globalization of the semiconductor supply chain and hardware trojan insertion phases.*

Despite their architectural efficiency, modern NoCs face severe security vulnerabilities arising from the globalization of the semiconductor design and fabrication ecosystem [3]. Semiconductor design houses increasingly rely on third-party intellectual property (IP) cores, outsourced electronic design automation (EDA) software, and untrusted foreign fabrication foundries [4]. This fragmented supply chain creates dangerous opportunities for adversaries to implant malicious, covert microarchitectural circuits known as **Hardware Trojans (HTs)** [3, 5]. Because an NoC router serves as the central conduit for inter-core communication, a single compromised router can compromise the integrity, confidentiality, and availability of the entire multicore chip [5].

## 1.2 Existing System and Microarchitectural Vulnerabilities

Conventional Network-on-Chip architectures are engineered under the fundamental assumption of **implicit hardware trust** [5]. In a standard 2D mesh, routers utilize minimalist Dimension-Order Routing (DOR)—such as deterministic XY routing—because it guarantees deadlock freedom with minimal logic overhead [2]. However, this rigidity introduces severe vulnerabilities when confronted with adversarial silicon behavior:

1. **Deterministic Single-Point Vulnerability:** Under baseline XY routing, the path traversed by a packet is entirely predetermined by the coordinate difference $(\Delta x, \Delta y)$ between source and destination [2]. If an intermediate router on this trajectory contains a hardware trojan, every communication flow intersecting that router is captured and disrupted without alternative traversal options [5].
2. **Absence of Microarchitectural Isolation:** Standard NoC routers possess no native capability to isolate compromised nodes [4]. When an anomalous router is identified by system monitors, existing architectures cannot reroute transit traffic around the faulty switch without inducing deadlocks, circular routing loops, or illegal diagonal hops [6].
3. **Simulation Non-Determinism via RNG Coupling:** A critical, pervasive flaw identified in existing discrete-event simulation studies (such as upstream Noxim) is **Random Number Generator (RNG) coupling** [1]. In standard simulators, global C library pseudo-random numbers (`rand()`) are shared concurrently between packet injection (`canShot()`) and router arbitration (`txProcess()`). When an attack (such as Blackhole) drops packets, downstream router contention decreases, reducing arbitration `rand()` calls. Consequently, the global random sequence shifts out of phase, causing the simulator to inject completely different packets with different timestamps in subsequent cycles [1]. This coupling renders comparative security evaluations statistically invalid because the baseline and attack runs evaluate different traffic workloads.

## 1.3 Proposed System: The NoC Sentinel Platform

To address the critical shortcomings of existing architectures and simulation practices, this project presents **NoC Sentinel**, an open-source, evidence-grounded cybersecurity and mitigation platform for Networks-on-Chip. NoC Sentinel operates across four tightly integrated tiers:

## 2.3 Research Gaps Identified in Prior Academic Work

A rigorous evaluation of prior literature reveals three critical research gaps:
1. **The Simulation Non-Determinism Trap:** Almost all published NoC security papers using discrete-event simulators fail to decouple traffic generation from arbitration. Consequently, their reported comparative delivery rates reflect statistical simulation artifacts rather than true mitigation effectiveness.
2. **Deadlock vs Adaptivity Dilemma in Avoidance Routing:** Existing turn-model defenses prohibit specific turns globally, which severely cripples throughput when an interior node fails. Table-driven methods require expensive hardware memory. A lightweight algorithm that provably avoids loops without global turn constraints was lacking.
3. **Unvalidated Machine Learning Claims:** Recent literature frequently reports 99–100% accuracy using machine learning for hardware security without testing cross-topology domain shift. Models trained on raw packet counts from $8 \times 8$ meshes fail completely when deployed on $4 \times 4$ systems.

## 3.1 Proposed Methodology and Theoretical Graph Modeling

The core design philosophy of NoC Sentinel is **evidence-based mathematical rigor**. Rather than relying on heuristic rerouting rules that risk circular wait states, Sentinel models the 2D mesh as a formal directed graph $G = (V, E)$ [2]:
- **Vertex Set $V$:** Represents the set of 16 router-PE tiles: $V = \{0, 1, 2, \dots, 15\}$ arranged as an $X \times Y$ grid ($X=4, Y=4$).
- **Edge Set $E$:** Represents bidirectional physical channels connecting cardinal neighbors:
$$E = \{(u, v) \mid |x_u - x_v| + |y_u - y_v| = 1\}$$

```
    Node 0 (0,0) ===== Node 1 (1,0) ===== Node 2 (2,0) ===== Node 3 (3,0)
         ||                 ||                 ||                 ||
    Node 4 (0,1) ===== Node 5 (1,1) ===== Node 6 (2,1) ===== Node 7 (3,1)
         ||                 ||                 ||                 ||
    Node 8 (0,2) ===== Node 9 (1,2) ===== Node 10 (2,2) ==== Node 11 (3,2)
         ||                 ||                 ||                 ||
    Node 12 (0,3) ==== Node 13 (1,3) ==== Node 14 (2,3) ==== Node 15 (3,3)
```
*Figure 3.1: Standard $4 \times 4$ 2D Mesh topology layout with cardinal port designations.*

```
             Turn 1: (East -> North)           Turn 2: (West -> South)
                  ^                                 |
                  |                                 v
             +----+                            <----+
             |                                      |
         East Traffic                           West Traffic
```
*Figure 3.2: Dimension-Order (XY) routing traversal and turn model constraints.*

### Punctured Graph Formulation:
When router $N \in V$ is compromised, Sentinel isolates node $N$ by puncturing the graph:
$$G' = G \setminus \{N\} = (V \setminus \{N\}, E')$$
where $E' = \{(u, v) \in E \mid u \neq N \land v \neq N\}$.

### Theorem 1 (Deadlock- and Loop-Freedom of Sentinel BFS Avoidance Routing):
*Let $d_{G'}(u, D)$ denote the shortest topological hop distance from current router $u$ to destination router $D$ on the punctured graph $G'$. If a routing algorithm selects an output port leading to an adjacent neighbor $v \in \text{Adj}(u)$ such that:*
$$d_{G'}(v, D) < d_{G'}(u, D)$$
*then the routing path contains no loops and guarantees delivery in at most $d_{G'}(\text{src}, D)$ hops.*

*Proof:* Assume for contradiction that a packet traverses a routing loop $C = (u_0, u_1, \dots, u_k = u_0)$. By definition of the routing rule:
$$d_{G'}(u_0, D) > d_{G'}(u_1, D) > \dots > d_{G'}(u_k, D) = d_{G'}(u_0, D)$$
This implies $d_{G'}(u_0, D) > d_{G'}(u_0, D)$, which is a strict mathematical impossibility. Thus, no circular paths can exist. Furthermore, because $G'$ remains connected for all single-node failures in a $4 \times 4$ mesh (minimum degree of remaining nodes $\ge 2$), every packet reaches its destination in finite steps. $\blacksquare$

### 4.2.2 Algorithm 2: Cycle-Free BFS Avoidance Routing on $G \setminus \{N\}$ (`SecurityTrace.cpp`)
When Sentinel mitigation is active (`NOXIM_MITIGATE=1`), neighbor routers intercept packets whose default XY path traverses compromised router $N$ and compute detour routes using Breadth-First Search on $G \setminus \{N\}$.

```
Algorithm 2: Cycle-Free BFS Shortest-Path Avoidance Routing on G \ {N}
Input: current_router u, destination D, compromised_router N, mesh_dims (X, Y)
Output: Next adjacent router v in Adj(u) \ {N}

1: if u == D then return LOCAL end if
2: 
3: // 1. Check if default XY path intersects compromised router N
4: default_next <- ComputeXYNextHop(u, D, X, Y)
5: if default_next != N and N not in ComputeXYPath(default_next, D)[1:-1] then
6:     return default_next
7: end if
8: 
9: // 2. Perform BFS from destination D on punctured graph G \ {N}
10: Queue Q <- empty
11: Map dist <- empty, dist[D] <- 0
12: Q.push(D)
13: while not Q.empty() do
14:     curr <- Q.pop()
15:     for each nbr in CardinalNeighbors(curr, X, Y) do
16:         if nbr != N and nbr not in dist then
17:             dist[nbr] <- dist[curr] + 1
18:             Q.push(nbr)
19:         end if
20:     end for
21: end while
22: 
23: // 3. Select neighbor strictly minimizing distance to D
24: best_nbr <- null, min_d <- infinity
25: for each nbr in CardinalNeighbors(u, X, Y) do
26:     if nbr != N and nbr in dist then
27:         if dist[nbr] < dist[u] and dist[nbr] < min_d then
28:             min_d <- dist[nbr]
29:             best_nbr <- nbr
30:         end if
31:     end if
32: end for
33: 
34: RecordDeviation(u, p.id, default_next, best_nbr, "MITIGATION")
35: return best_nbr
```

## 4.3 Configurable Ingress/Egress Endpoint Policy Engine

When a router is compromised, its local Processing Element is untrusted, and legitimate traffic destined for that core is vulnerable to interception. Sentinel implements four endpoint policies:

```
                            +--------------------------+
                            | Packet Generated at Core |
                            +--------------------------+
                                          |
                        [Is Sentinel Mitigation Active?]
                                       /                                      NO   YES
                                     /                               [Inject Normally] +----------------------------+
                                            | Evaluate Ingress / Egress  |
                                            +----------------------------+
                                            /                                                 [Source is Compromised Node N?]      [Destination is Compromised Node N?]
                                   / \                                    / |                             ALLOW   QUARANTINE                    DELIVER  |  REMAP
                             /           \                         /       |                       [Inject into Mesh]   [QUARANTINED_SRC]  [Deliver to N]     |   [Rewrite Dst -> Backup Core]
                                      [Discard at Source]                   |   [Deliver safely over G \ {N}]
                                                                            |
                                                                   [QUARANTINED_DST]
                                                                   [Discard at Source]
```
*Figure 4.3: Destination remapping workflow redirecting traffic from Node 6 to Backup Node 2.*

1. **`QUARANTINE` (Default Isolation Policy):**
   - **Source Quarantine:** Packets generated by $PE_N$ are intercepted in `onPacketGenerated()`, labeled `QUARANTINED_SRC`, and discarded before entering the input buffer.
   - **Destination Quarantine:** Packets addressed to $N$ are intercepted at the source NI, labeled `QUARANTINED_DST`, and discarded to prevent data exfiltration.
2. **`REMAP` (Backup Redirection Policy):**
   - When a packet's logical destination is $N$, Sentinel rewrites the packet header to backup core $B$ (`NOXIM_BACKUP_NODE`):
   $$p.\text{actual\_dst} \leftarrow B, \quad p.\text{remapped} \leftarrow \text{true}$$
   - The packet is injected into the network, routed around $N$ over $G \setminus \{N\}$, and safely delivered to backup core $B$.
3. **`DELIVER` / `ALLOW`:**
   - Permissive research baseline modes that allow packets to enter or exit node $N$ to quantify unmitigated vulnerability.

The simulation engine exports cycle-accurate telemetry to `<run>_packets.csv` and `<run>_hops.csv`:

# CHAPTER 6: CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE

## 6.1 Conclusion

In this major project, we designed, implemented, and comprehensively verified **NoC Sentinel**, an open-source, evidence-grounded cybersecurity and mitigation platform for Networks-on-Chip. Operating within the cycle-accurate SystemC/Noxim simulation environment, Sentinel addresses the critical threat posed by microarchitectural hardware trojans embedded within router control logic. By modeling five distinct attack prototypes—Blackhole, Selective Forwarding, Grayhole, Routing Manipulation, and Destination Tampering—the platform establishes a reproducible experimental baseline for evaluating on-chip cybersecurity resilience.

The core contribution of this work lies in the formulation and verification of a provably loop-free Breadth-First Search (BFS) avoidance routing protocol over the punctured mesh graph $G \setminus \{N\}$. By enforcing strictly decreasing topological distances to destinations, Sentinel mathematically guarantees loop-freedom and deadlock prevention without resorting to restrictive global turn prohibitions or expensive hardware routing tables. Across an exhaustive 63-run test matrix on a $4 \times 4$ 2D mesh, Sentinel demonstrated 100.0% settled delivery recovery for all uncompromised traffic, restoring network throughput while recording exactly zero routing loops, zero invalid hops, and zero unauthorized intermediate visits to compromised routers.

Furthermore, Sentinel resolved the pervasive issue of simulation non-determinism in discrete-event network modeling by decoupling router arbitration pseudo-randomness into an independent $xorshift32$ stream, achieving 100.0% pairwise traffic injection equivalence across all comparative runs. The platform also conducted an honest, rigorous audit of machine-learning-based detection, transparently documenting a 100% false positive rate domain-shift failure in a pre-trained Random Forest classifier due to scale-dependent volume features, and delivered an interactive 6-tab Streamlit intelligence dashboard integrated with Google Gemini for grounded AI threat briefings. In summary, NoC Sentinel delivers a publication-grade, fully auditable cybersecurity foundation for safety-critical multicore Systems-on-Chip.

## 6.2 Future Scope

The successful implementation and empirical verification of NoC Sentinel open several promising avenues for future research and engineering development:

First, future research will explore the integration of lightweight, hardware-synthesizable credit-counter anomaly monitors directly at router Network Interfaces to achieve autonomous, real-time trojan discovery in silicon. By tracking packet inter-arrival distributions and credit return latencies locally, routers could autonomously identify malicious behavior and trigger avoidance reconfiguration without requiring prior knowledge handoff from an external monitoring plane.

Second, the avoidance routing framework can be extended to support simultaneous multi-node failures and complex topological partitions. By incorporating biconnected component decomposition algorithms and dynamic virtual channel partitioning, the network can maintain connectivity and deadlock freedom even under coordinated distributed hardware attacks or extreme localized thermal hotspot failures.

Third, the machine learning detection pipeline should be retrained on scale-invariant rate features—such as flits per cycle per active node, queue occupancy ratios, and normalized drop percentages—across diverse mesh dimensions ($4 \times 4, 8 \times 8, 16 \times 16$) and traffic distributions (e.g., hotspot, transpose, self-similar workloads). This will eliminate scale-dependent feature traps and establish true cross-topology model transferability.

Finally, future efforts will target physical FPGA emulation and ASIC synthesis. Translating Sentinel's BFS avoidance logic and decoupled arbitration into synthesizable SystemVerilog Register-Transfer Level (RTL) and deploying the design onto a Xilinx UltraScale+ FPGA platform will enable precise measurement of physical silicon area overhead, critical path timing degradation, and dynamic switching power in modern semiconductor technology nodes.

### ABSTRACT
*Network-on-Chip (NoC) constitutes the primary communication backbone for high-performance Multiprocessor Systems-on-Chip (MPSoCs). However, the shared nature of on-chip routers and links exposes modern architectures to hardware Trojans, malicious routing modifications, and Denial-of-Service (DoS) exploits such as blackhole and grayhole packet-dropping attacks. This paper presents **NoC Sentinel**, an evidence-based cybersecurity and automated mitigation platform designed for 2D mesh on-chip networks. Built upon cycle-accurate SystemC simulation in Noxim, NoC Sentinel introduces an operational framework comprising: (i) an Empirical Telemetry Engine that captures packet-level flit timings, hop trajectories, and buffer utilization; (ii) a Dynamic BFS Deflection Rerouting algorithm that bypasses identified adversary nodes without hardware router modification; (iii) a Policy-Driven Destination Remapping engine that redirects targeted transactions to designated fallback nodes; and (iv) an AI-assisted Security Analyst interface that cross-examines telemetry against empirical bounds. Experimental evaluation on a $4 \times 4$ mesh under Poisson traffic demonstrates that blackhole attacks on transit nodes cause up to 15.24% network-wide packet loss (dropping 100% of traversing traffic), while grayhole attacks silently discard 50% of forwarded packets. The deployment of NoC Sentinel's dynamic deflection and remapping policies completely eliminates packet dropping, restoring settled packet delivery to 100.0% with a modest, bounded mean latency overhead (e.g., 26.24 cycles baseline to 28.32 cycles under BFS bypass). We also critically evaluate machine learning intrusion detection on NoC telemetry, detailing the operational impact of feature volume shifts.*

***Keywords***—*Network-on-Chip (NoC), Hardware Security, Blackhole Attack, Grayhole Attack, Adaptive Deflection Routing, Breadth-First Search (BFS), SystemC, Multi-Core SoC.*

### I. INTRODUCTION
Modern Systems-on-Chip (SoCs) integrate tens to hundreds of heterogeneous Intellectual Property (IP) cores interconnected via Network-on-Chip (NoC) architectures [1], [2]. Compared to traditional shared bus interconnects, NoCs provide scalable bandwidth, reduced wiring congestion, and predictable electrical timing through packetized wormhole switching. 

Despite their performance advantages, modern semiconductor globalization has introduced critical security vulnerabilities. Deep sub-micron fabrication relies on untrusted third-party IP vendors and multi-facility offshore foundries, creating opportunities for malicious hardware Trojans and firmware modifications [4], [5]. A compromised router can execute disruptive Denial-of-Service (DoS) attacks:
- **Blackhole Attacks:** Malicious routers drop all flits routed through them, isolating downstream destination cores and causing critical task starvation [8].
- **Grayhole Attacks:** Malicious routers selectively drop packets (e.g., alternating packets or specific transaction types), remaining stealthy while degrading Quality-of-Service (QoS) [4].
- **Target Subversion:** Compromised nodes disrupt service by rejecting or misrouting incoming transactions destined for critical memory or acceleration cores.

Existing NoC security schemes often mandate substantial hardware modifications, such as dedicated cryptographic wrappers, duplicate physical links, or complex anomaly hardware [6], [7]. These approaches impose prohibitive area, power, and latency penalties. 

To address these limitations, we present **NoC Sentinel**, an evidence-based cybersecurity and automated mitigation platform. NoC Sentinel operates at the supervisory and network routing layer, achieving zero-loss recovery against blackhole, grayhole, and destination compromise attacks without altering the underlying physical router pipeline.

### II. NETWORK ARCHITECTURE AND THREAT MODEL

#### A. Target 2D Mesh Architecture
We model a 16-node ($4 \times 4$) 2D mesh interconnect implemented in cycle-accurate SystemC using the Noxim simulator [3]. Each tile $N_{i,j}$ (where $x \in [0,3], y \in [0,3]$) incorporates an IP processing core, a network interface (NI), and a 5-port wormhole router (Local, North, East, South, West). 
- **Flit Structure:** 8 flits per packet (1 Head flit carrying routing header, 6 Body flits carrying payload, 1 Tail flit releasing virtual channels).
- **Default Routing:** Deterministic Dimension-Order XY routing, where flits traverse the X-axis completely before turning onto the Y-axis.
- **Traffic Model:** Poisson packet injection across non-reserved cores at an injection rate of 0.01 packets/cycle/node.

#### B. Adversarial Threat Model
We formalize three attack scenarios embedded within the router microarchitecture:
1. **Node 6 Transit Blackhole ($A_1$):** Router 6 ($(x=2, y=1)$) is modified to drop 100% of traversing packets whose destination $d \neq 6$. Packets destined locally to Node 6 are accepted.
2. **Node 10 Transit Grayhole ($A_2$):** Router 10 ($(x=2, y=2)$) drops exactly every second packet ($k \pmod 2 == 1$) traversing its switch fabric, silently discarding 50% of transit flits.
3. **Node 6 Destination Subversion ($A_3$):** Node 6 acts as a compromised sink, rendering any service requests directed to it corrupt or unresponsive.

### III. MITIGATION ENGINE DESIGN

NoC Sentinel implements a two-stage mitigation policy that dynamically adapts the routing matrix upon threat identification:

#### A. Dynamic BFS Deflection Rerouting
To bypass compromised routers without physical wire replacement, the supervisory routing layer executes a dynamic Breadth-First Search (BFS) over the sub-graph $G' = (V \setminus \{A\}, E)$, where $A$ denotes the set of identified adversary nodes.

```
Algorithm 1: Dynamic BFS Deflection Rerouting
Input: Source s, Destination d, Compromised Router Set A, Graph G=(V,E)
Output: Valid loop-free path P
1:  if s == d then return [s]
2:  Initialize queue Q with path [s]
3:  Initialize visited set V_vis = {s} U A
4:  while Q is not empty do
5:      path = Q.pop_front()
6:      u = path.back()
7:      if u == d then return path
8:      for each neighbor v in G.neighbors(u) do
9:          if v not in V_vis then
10:             V_vis.add(v)
11:             Q.push_back(path + [v])
12: return FAIL_NO_PATH
```

For instance, under standard XY routing, traffic from Node 4 $(0,1)$ to Node 7 $(3,1)$ traverses the deterministic path $4 \to 5 \to 6 \to 7$. In the presence of a blackhole at Node 6, Algorithm 1 dynamically deflects packets along the alternate loop-free trajectory $4 \to 8 \to 9 \to 10 \to 7$ or $4 \to 0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 7$, completely avoiding the compromised switch fabric.

#### B. Policy-Driven Destination Remapping
When a destination node itself is compromised ($A_3$), deflection routing cannot prevent transaction failure. NoC Sentinel invokes an autonomous remapping rule:
$$\text{Remap}(d) = \begin{cases} d_{\text{backup}}, & \text{if } d \in A_{\text{dest}} \\ d, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
The network interface updates the Head flit destination address at the ingress boundary, seamlessly retargeting packets (e.g., from Node 6 to designated backup Node 2) while maintaining packet ordering and delivery verification.

#### A. Experimental Setup
Simulations were executed across six standardized runs (Warmup: 2,000 cycles; Active simulation: 4,000 cycles; Total: 6,000 cycles; Drain margin: 500 cycles). Packets injected within the stable active window $[2000, 5500]$ constitute the settled evaluation set $S$.

#### C. Key Empirical Findings
1. **Blackhole Impact and Zero-Loss Recovery:** Under unmitigated blackhole operation at Node 6, exactly 87 settled packets traversing Node 6 are dropped, reducing delivery to 84.76%. Activating Dynamic BFS Deflection restores delivery to exactly 571/571 packets (100.0%), incurring a negligible latency increase of only 2.08 cycles due to the additional hop deflection.
2. **Grayhole Detection and Mitigation:** Node 10 grayhole dropping silently discards 26 settled packets (95.45% delivery). BFS bypass successfully reroutes all 26 packets around Node 10, achieving 100.0% delivery with 27.94 cycles mean latency.
3. **Destination Remapping Precision:** Retargeting Node 6 traffic to Node 2 resulted in exactly 40/40 settled packets (and 53/53 total measured packets) being delivered successfully to Node 2, completely neutralizing sink compromise.
4. **Machine Learning Feature Shift Analysis:** We evaluated a Random Forest classifier trained on single-link synthetic features. When tested against $4 \times 4$ multi-link NoC telemetry, the model exhibited a high False Positive Rate due to a feature volume threshold split ($pkts \le 1487$). This rigorously demonstrates why hardware NoC intrusion detection systems must incorporate structural topological metrics rather than localized raw packet counts alone.

### V. CONCLUSION
NoC Sentinel provides an evidence-based, cycle-accurate framework for evaluating and mitigating cybersecurity threats in multi-core on-chip networks. By decoupling defensive policies from physical router silicon modifications, the platform achieves zero-loss packet delivery recovery against blackhole, grayhole, and destination attacks with minimal latency overhead. The open-source integration of SystemC telemetry with modern interactive dashboards offers a robust foundation for future research in autonomous, AI-driven on-chip security management.
